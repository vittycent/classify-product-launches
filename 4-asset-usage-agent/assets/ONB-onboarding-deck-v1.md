_For CSMs: run slides 1 to 15 at every kickoff. Use the appendix (16 to 19) when the customer has more than one entity or brand. Speaker notes are for you, not the customer._

---

### S01: Welcome to Rootline

**On the slide**

- [Customer name] and Rootline
- Kickoff, [date]
- Today: who does what, what happens in the next four weeks, and where we need you

**Speaker notes**

Start by asking what success looks like for them at the end of the first project. Write it down in their words; you will use it at the first QBR.

---

### S02: Your team

**On the slide**

- **Your customer success manager:** your contact for as long as you are a customer, not just during onboarding
- **Your solutions engineer:** builds your model with you
- **Your account executive:** stays in the loop on anything commercial
- Tell us early when something isn't working, rather than saving it for a review

**Speaker notes**

Customers are used to being passed from team to team. Say plainly that you are staying.

---

**Speaker notes**

Delete the column that isn't theirs and fill in their add ons from the order form before the call. Walk through the allowances once, calmly: a customer who discovers a limit halfway through a project feels misled, even when it was in the contract. For customers still on a legacy module contract, skip this slide and describe their modules instead.

### S04: A joint project

**On the slide**

- Buying Rootline gives you the model, the factors, the system and our team. It doesn't mean we do the work for you
- **We do:** clean your data, build the model, draft the supplier request, run the reminders, prepare your draft numbers, and tell you early when something slips
- **You do:** bring in your own people (production, purchasing, R&D, finance), contact your suppliers, follow up with the ones that matter, and make the decisions only you can make
- Your suppliers know you, not us. The request, the relationship and the follow up are yours

**Speaker notes**

Say this at kickoff, plainly and early. Projects stall when a customer expects us to reach their suppliers or chase their colleagues for them. We can't, and it wouldn't work if we did: suppliers answer the people they already do business with.

---

### S05: Onboarding at a glance

**On the slide**

- Kickoff within two weeks of signature
- **Weeks 1 to 4: your first numbers**
  - Week 1: we clean your data
  - Week 2: we build the model
  - Week 3: you review the draft numbers
  - Week 4: buffer, because something always comes up in the first three
- **Weeks 1 to 6: your core suppliers**, onboarded, and a plan for the rest
- **Week 6: onboarding closes** (slide 14)

**Speaker notes**

The buffer week is honest, not padding. If a project is going to slip, you will know by week 2. Tell the customer then, not in week 4.

---

### S06: Three decisions only you can make

**On the slide**

1. **Your data:** a year of purchase data and your recipes, in week 1
2. **Your supplier letter:** approve the wording before anything is sent
3. **Your draft review:** two hours with the draft numbers in week 3

- Alongside these, your team runs the conversations with your suppliers (slide 9)

**Speaker notes**

These are the three moments nobody else can stand in for. Don't let the slide read as "three meetings and we do the rest": the supplier work on slide 9 is real work on their side, and it runs through all six weeks.

---

### S07: The data we need from you

**On the slide**

- **Purchase data, twelve months:** supplier, material, quantity with a unit, and value
- **Recipes or specifications** for the products in scope
- **Your supplier list,** with a contact for each
- Quantities matter: with value alone, we can only do a spend based estimate for that line

**Speaker notes**

Ask them to check recipes with production, not only with the system. Specifications and practice drift apart over the years, and week 3 is an expensive place to find out.

---

### S08: The draft review

**On the slide**

- The most important two hours of the project
- You know things about your production that are not in any system
- Most of them only come out when you look at a number and it looks wrong
- Bring whoever knows the factory best

**Speaker notes**

Book the review at kickoff, not in week 3. It is the step that gets squeezed when calendars fill up, and it is the one that catches the errors.

---

### S09: Reaching your suppliers

**On the slide**

- With the supplier engagement add on, we give you the template, the system and the reminders
- **Your team sends the request,** in your company's name, and follows up with the suppliers that matter. Suppliers answer the companies they sell to, not a software vendor they have never heard of
- We draft the letter from our template, you rewrite it in your own words
- Nothing goes to a supplier until you have approved the wording
- Every supplier who answers gets their own figure back

**Speaker notes**

Open the supplier outreach template (TPL) on screen and go through it with them. Most customers change a few lines, which is exactly what we want. Ask who on their side owns each supplier relationship; that person, not the sustainability lead, often needs to make the call.

---

### S10: What we ask a dairy farm

**On the slide**

- Six fields:
  1. Feed composition
  2. Manure management system
  3. Soil type
  4. Herd size
  5. Milk yield
  6. Any manure treatment
- Soil type is the one with the widest spread. Drained peat changes everything
- For other categories the fields change. The principle doesn't: ask only for what moves the number

**Speaker notes**

We added soil type in this version. Until now it came from soil maps, which describe a parish, not a field.

---

### S11: Core suppliers first, then waves

**On the slide**

- **Core suppliers, during onboarding:** the one to three suppliers that matter most. A big share of your footprint, and a relationship strong enough to get a quick yes
- **Wave two, after onboarding:** the rest of your first block, following your plan, with a better letter and one line saying how many of their peers have already replied
- **Then the long tail,** through the system
- Get it right with a few before you send to many

**Speaker notes**

Agree the core suppliers at kickoff and write their names down. For a dairy with a hundred farms, "core" means a handful of the largest farms. One block covers 25 active suppliers a year, so plan the waves inside it, and talk to the customer about a second block before wave two, not after.

---

### S12: What response to expect

**On the slide**

- Your core suppliers: we aim for all of them returning data by week 6
- A typical wave after that: **25 to 40 percent** of suppliers reply with usable data
- Response improves every year, as more of their customers ask
- Our chase sequence: automatic reminders at day 7, 14 and 21, then a call from you for the ones that matter most
- Some suppliers will never reply. That is normal

**Speaker notes**

Set the expectation at kickoff so a 30 percent wave feels like success, because it is. Anything above 40 is excellent.

---

### S13: Suppliers who don't reply

**On the slide**

- They stay in your footprint on a regional average, clearly marked as such
- Every line in your report shows whether it is primary data, a regional average or a global average
- You improve them next year, when the question has had time to land

**Speaker notes**

Customers worry that gaps make the report weak. The opposite is true: labelled gaps are what make the report credible.

---

### S14: When onboarding is done

**On the slide**

Onboarding closes when all three are true:

1. **Your first product footprints are delivered and reviewed** (week 4)
2. **Your core suppliers are onboarded:** each has agreed to share and returned its first data (by week 6)
3. **You have a written plan for the rest of your suppliers:** who, in which wave, who contacts them on your side, and by when

- After that, the rest of your suppliers follow the plan, and we track progress at your reviews

**Speaker notes**

Close onboarding on these three criteria, not when every supplier has replied. Supplier responses take months; onboarding shouldn't. If a customer reaches their first review still "in onboarding", something went wrong in the first six weeks: the first review should be about results and the plan, not about finishing the setup.

---

### S15: After go live

**On the slide**

- **Reviews with your CSM.** Supply: a check in after onboarding, a conversation mid year and a review before renewal. Group: a quarterly business review
- **Your supplier plan** is a standing item at every review
- **Month six:** we train a second person on your side, so the knowledge doesn't live with one person
- Your next customer request should take days, not months

**Speaker notes**

Book the month six session at kickoff and put a name against it. The single biggest risk on most accounts is one person holding everything.

---

## Appendix: groups with more than one entity or brand

### S16: Roll out in waves

**On the slide**

- Start with your four largest brands: usually about **60 percent of your footprint**, live in **eight to ten weeks**
- The next brands in the following two quarters
- The tail after that
- A defensible group number inside one reporting cycle

**Speaker notes**

Push back if a customer wants every entity at once. Parallel onboardings run by one person produce a set of weak results instead of a few strong ones.

---

### S17: Start with the entity most likely to resist

**On the slide**

- If an entity has its own sustainability person, or its own published figures, start there, not last
- Brought in first, they are a participant, not a subject
- Tell them the project exists this week, not in month six
- Their existing work is an input, not something being replaced

**Speaker notes**

Resistance usually comes from hearing about the project second hand, not from the order you choose.

---

### S18: Your material codes stay as they are

**On the slide**

- We connect to each entity's purchase ledger and material master
- The mapping happens in our layer. You don't harmonise anything first
- New materials land in a review queue: a few hours a week in the first quarter, then 15 to 30 minutes a month
- Split the queue by entity, so each purchasing team maps its own materials

**Speaker notes**

It also gives purchasing a stake in the output from the start, which helps later when you want them to act on it.

---

### S19: Last year's numbers

**On the slide**

- If last year's underlying data still exists, we load it as a comparison year
- If it doesn't, this year becomes your base year
- Moving a base year is normal. Decide it now, and write down why

**Speaker notes**

Re basing in the year you change system is easy to explain. Doing it a year later is much harder.
