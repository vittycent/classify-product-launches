## 1. The questionnaire got longer

If you supply a grocer or a brand, you already know the supplier questionnaire: food safety, certifications, packaging, ethics. What's new is the carbon section. A year ago it was a couple of questions. This year, for many producers, it runs to pages.

It usually lands with whoever looks after quality or the environment, on top of everything else they do. If that's you, this post is for you.

## 2. Didn't the reporting rules just change?

They did. This spring the thresholds for sustainability reporting were raised, and many smaller and mid sized producers are now out of scope, or in a later wave.

That doesn't mean the questionnaire goes away. The retailers and brands you sell to are often still reporting, and your products are part of their footprint. Their obligation reaches you as a request from a customer, and a customer's request doesn't depend on whether you're in scope.

## 3. Work out what it's actually asking

Before you fill in a single box, answer four questions about the request itself:

- **Product or company?** A footprint per product and a footprint for your whole company are very different pieces of work. Many templates ask for one and mention the other
- **Which products?** All of them, your best sellers, or the ones in a specific range? If it isn't clear, ask. The difference in effort is large
- **Which method will they accept?** Some templates say so. If yours doesn't, ask whether an estimate based on what you spend is enough for this year
- **By when?** Write the date down, and check whether it's a deadline or a suggestion

## 4. How much weight does it carry?

Not every scorecard is equally urgent. Three signs tell you how seriously to take one:

1. **A deadline**
2. **A named person** on the retailer's side who will read your answer
3. **A consequence:** it's clear what happens if you don't answer, or answer badly

A request with all three is part of how that customer will judge you as a supplier. A request with only a date is often a form that has to go out to every supplier this year. Both deserve an honest answer. Only the first deserves a project.

If you can't tell, ask your contact at the retailer what happens to the answers. It's a normal question, and the reply tells you a lot.

## 5. Answering this year

Most retailer templates this year still accept a number built from what you spend, with standard database factors, as long as you say how you calculated it. For a first scorecard, that is often a reasonable answer.

A few things make it a good one:

- **Say how you got the number.** One or two sentences on method and sources, in the template, not in a separate email
- **Mark what is estimated.** Buyers are used to estimates. What costs you trust is a figure that looks more certain than it is
- **Keep your workings.** Next year's template will ask what changed, and you will want to know

## 6. Getting ready for next year

Templates are tightening. The direction is clear, even if the timing isn't: product level numbers, methods based on what you actually buy and make rather than what you spend, and a growing share of data from your own suppliers.

If you expect to keep answering, it pays to build once:

- **Start with your first request,** usually product footprints for the products your customer asked about
- **Plan for about ten hours** from the person who knows how your products are really made, at the start. First product footprints typically take about four weeks
- **Build the company footprint from the same data** when the next request asks for it, rather than starting again
- **Look at supplier data last,** and only for the ingredients that carry most of your number

## 7. A checklist for your first scorecard

- Do you know whether it asks for product or company numbers, and for which products?
- Do you know the deadline, and whether it's firm?
- Is there a named person and a clear consequence behind it?
- Have you stated your method in the template itself?
- Have you marked which figures are estimates?
- Have you kept your workings for next year?

## Key takeaways

- The reporting rules moved; your customers' requests didn't
- A deadline, a named person and a consequence tell you how seriously to take a scorecard
- This year, an honest estimate with its method stated is usually enough. Next year may ask for more
