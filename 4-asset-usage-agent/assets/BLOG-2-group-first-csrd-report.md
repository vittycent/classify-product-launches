## 1. A group report is a different animal

If you run sustainability for a food group, your first CSRD report is not one footprint. It is five, twelve or thirty footprints, one for every entity and brand, built on different systems, sometimes with different ideas of what a "product" is. Many of those entities arrived through acquisitions, and some came with a footprint of their own that nobody has checked against yours.

Then someone independent has to look at it. For most groups, the first report comes with limited assurance, and the assurance provider will want to understand how every number was built.

The good news is that their questions are predictable. Here are the five we see most often, and what a good answer looks like. But first, the idea that sits under all of them.

## 2. There is no single right number

Ask three vendors to calculate the same group's footprint and you will get three different answers. Each will use different datasets, draw the boundary in a slightly different place, and make different calls on allocation, land use and missing data. None of them is necessarily wrong.

So the goal of a CSRD report isn't to find the one right number. It is to have a number that is right for your business, and that you can stand behind:

- **Defensible:** every figure traces back to data and choices you can show
- **True to your business:** the datasets, boundaries and methods fit how your group actually sources, produces and sells, rather than a generic average
- **Explainable:** when someone questions a figure, or a claim you have built on it, you can defend the reasoning, not just the result

This is where experience matters. Rootline has built footprints for food producers and groups across the Nordics. We know which data holds up in a CSRD process, where the judgement calls sit, and how to document them so they survive scrutiny. What we give you isn't a number handed over the table. It is a number you understand well enough to defend, with the context around it: your acquisitions, your suppliers, your customers' expectations and the rules as they stand today.

Everything below comes back to this.

## 3. "Where did this number come from?"

Your assurance provider will pick a line, often a large one, and ask to follow it back to its source. What was bought, in what quantity, from which system extract, and which emission factor was applied to it.

A good answer takes a few clicks, not a few days. For every factor, you should be able to show its **source, its vintage, its geography and its method**. If a figure was adjusted by hand, you should be able to show who changed it, when and why.

## 4. "What's in, and what's out?"

Scope 3 has fifteen categories. For a food group, purchased goods will dominate, usually somewhere between 80 and 95 percent of the total, and several categories will barely apply.

"Barely applies" still needs an answer. Every category should be **screened**, and every exclusion documented with a reason and a name. A category left blank looks like something you forgot. A category marked "not applicable, because..." looks like a decision.

## 5. "What changed since last year, and why?"

This is the question that catches groups out. Your footprint will move between years for three very different reasons:

1. **Factor updates:** the emission factors themselves were revised
2. **Activity corrections:** your own data was fixed or completed
3. **Methodology changes:** you changed how something is calculated

Each is disclosed differently, and a board reads each differently. A restatement that only gives the total invites questions. One that splits the total into its three causes answers them before they are asked.

## 6. "How much of this is measured?"

Assurance providers want to know how much of your footprint rests on real supplier data and how much on averages.

Here's the trap: **measure coverage by share of emissions, not by number of lines.** A food group's purchase ledger is full of small, immaterial lines. Counted by lines, your coverage will look terrible, or, if you count the other way, flatteringly good. Counted by emissions, it tells you where the real gaps are, which is usually a handful of large suppliers.

## 7. "Could anyone have changed this after the fact?"

For a first report, this is the question behind all the others. Once a reporting period is closed, its numbers and the factors used to calculate them should be **locked**. Changes should be logged, with the original kept alongside, and nobody, whatever their role, should be able to overwrite the history quietly.

## 8. Getting the group data in

Most first reports stall on data, not on methodology. Three things help:

- **Don't harmonise first.** Asking every entity to clean up its material codes before you start is how projects die. Map them centrally instead, and let each entity keep its own system
- **Start where the footprint is.** Your largest brands usually make up most of the number. Get them right first, then bring in the rest in waves
- **Decide your base year early.** If an acquired entity's past data can't be recalculated, make this year the base year and write down why

## 9. A checklist for your first group report

- Can you trace any number to its source, factor, vintage and method in minutes?
- Is every Scope 3 category screened, with exclusions documented?
- Can you split any year on year change into factor, activity and methodology?
- Do you report coverage by share of emissions?
- Are closed periods locked, with a change log nobody can edit?
- Do your largest brands come first in the rollout?
- Is your base year decided and documented?

If you can tick all seven, your assurance provider is going to have a short, pleasant engagement.
