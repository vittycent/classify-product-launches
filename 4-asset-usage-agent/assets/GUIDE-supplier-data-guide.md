## 1. Why this matters

In food, most of your footprint doesn't happen in your factory. It happens on the farms, orchards, vessels and processing sites that supply you. Until you have data from them, your footprint rests on averages, and averages can't show a customer, an auditor or your own buyers what is really going on in your supply chain.

Getting that data is the part most companies find hardest. This guide is about how to do it well.

## 2. Why most supplier requests fail

If you have already sent suppliers a questionnaire and heard very little back, you are not alone. A long questionnaire sent cold to the whole supplier list typically gets usable answers from fewer than one in five suppliers.

The reasons are almost always the same:

- **Too long.** Pages of questions, many of which don't apply to the supplier
- **Unfamiliar.** The request looks like paperwork from a stranger rather than a question from a customer they know
- **Nothing in return.** The supplier does the work, and gets nothing back for it
- **Everyone at once.** The same request goes to every supplier on the same day, so there's no chance to learn from the first replies
- **Lost in translation.** Your suppliers may be spread across several countries. A request in the wrong language, or full of terms that mean something different on the ground, gets set aside
- **The records don't exist yet.** Many farms don't have the software, the time or the complete records to answer every question. That isn't unwillingness, and it isn't something a reminder email will fix

Most of these are fixable. The last one needs a different approach: give suppliers grace. Ask for what they can realistically give, accept partial answers, and fill the gaps with assumptions that will hold up. That is where a partner with deep food data matters. Rootline's experience and data let you make credible assumptions about the missing pieces, so a partial answer still improves your number instead of stalling it.

## 3. Step one: rank your suppliers by emissions, not by spend

Your biggest supplier by spend is not necessarily your biggest by emissions. A supplier of expensive ingredients can be a small part of your footprint, and a supplier of cheap, bulky raw materials can be a large one.

Rank your suppliers by their estimated share of your emissions instead. For most food producers, the top dozen or so suppliers carry most of the footprint. That is where supplier data changes your number, and it is a far more manageable list than the whole ledger.

**Then look at each number in context.** A higher figure isn't automatically a worse supplier. An organic farm may carry a higher footprint per kilo while protecting soil health, biodiversity or a promise your customers are paying for. A number only means something next to what comparable farms achieve, and next to what matters in your market. Sometimes the right decision is to accept a higher figure, because the practice behind it supports your brand further up the chain. Rootline helps you read each supplier's number against comparable farms in our data and against your own market, so you can tell a practice worth keeping from a problem worth solving.

## 4. Step two: start with a few core suppliers

Don't start with the whole top dozen. Start with one to three core suppliers: a large share of your footprint, and a relationship strong enough that they are likely to say yes.

A quick early result does two things. It shows you what works in your request before you send it more widely, and it gives you something to tell the next suppliers.

## 5. Step three: ask for as little as possible

The shorter the request, the more answers you get. Ask only for the handful of things that actually move the number, and nothing that doesn't. If one number from an invoice will do, ask for one number.

What that looks like varies by supplier:

| Supplier | What to ask for |
|---|---|
| Dairy farm | Feed composition, manure management, soil type, herd size, milk yield, any manure treatment |
| Crop grower | Crop, yield per hectare, fertiliser use, soil type, irrigation and drying energy |
| Fishing vessel | Fuel used in a year, and landed weight |
| Importer or trader | Origin by product, processing or drying method, any existing footprint, breakdown by shipment |

## 6. Step four: ask as yourself, and give something back

**Send it in your company's name.** Suppliers answer the customers they sell to. If a partner is collecting and processing the data for you, say so, and say who they are.

**Explain how their data is handled.** Where it is stored, what it is used for, that it won't be sold or shared with other buyers, and that they can ask for it to be deleted.

**Give something back.** Every supplier who answers should get their own figure in return, to keep and to use with any other customer who asks. This is the single biggest reason suppliers reply.

## 7. Step five: work in waves

After your core suppliers, move through the rest of your priority list in waves, not all at once.

- **Improve the request between waves.** You will want to change something once the first replies come back
- **Tell later waves who has already replied.** A single line saying how many of their peers have already answered often lifts response more than anything else you change
- **Remind, then call.** Automatic reminders after one, two and three weeks, then a personal call to the suppliers that matter most

## 8. What response to expect

- From your core suppliers: all of them, if you have chosen well
- From each wave after that: typically 25 to 40 percent returning usable data
- Year on year: response improves, because more of your suppliers' other customers start asking the same questions

Some suppliers will never reply. That is normal, and it is not a failure of your programme.

## 9. Suppliers who don't reply

They stay in your footprint, and they don't have to drag it down to a generic average. Rootline's database of food specific factors, built with our data partners, lets us make credible assumptions for the suppliers who don't reply: by region, by farming practice and by type of operation. Each one is clearly marked as an assumption, with the reasoning behind it, so you can stand behind the figure and any claim you make with it.

That is a large part of what you are buying: not only a way to collect data, but the expertise and data to make a credible number when the data doesn't come. Ask the non responders again next year, when the question has had time to land.

## 10. A checklist

- Have you ranked suppliers by share of emissions, not spend?
- Have you chosen one to three core suppliers to start with?
- Is your request short enough to answer in one sitting?
- Does it come from your company, and explain who is processing the data and how?
- Does every supplier get their own figure back?
- Have you planned the waves after your core suppliers?
- Are the gaps filled with credible, clearly marked assumptions rather than generic averages?
- Have you read each supplier's number in context before acting on it?
