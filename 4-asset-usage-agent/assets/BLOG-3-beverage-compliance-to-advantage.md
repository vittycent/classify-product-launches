## 1. It started as a reporting job

Bryggeri Kvarnen is a family owned Swedish brewery with a range of lagers, ales and seasonal beers, sold mostly in cans. Last autumn it faced its first CSRD reporting cycle, and with it a job nobody at the brewery had done before: a carbon footprint for every product in the range.

Like most breweries, Kvarnen had a working theory about where its emissions came from. Brewing is hot, energy hungry work, so the brewhouse seemed the obvious place to look.

The numbers said something else.

## 2. Where the footprint of a can of lager sits

When Kvarnen's product footprints came back, the split for its best selling lager looked like this:

| Source | Share of the footprint |
|---|---|
| Malt | About a third |
| Packaging, mostly the can | About a third |
| Energy in brewing | About a fifth |
| Distribution and smaller inputs | The rest |

Two things stood out. Packaging was as big as the malt. And the brewhouse, where most of the brewery's own effort had gone, was the smallest of the three.

This is a common pattern in beverages. Making aluminium takes a great deal of energy, and a can is a lot of metal for a few hundred millilitres of drink. For a brewery, the container can matter as much as what goes in it.

## 3. Per litre or per can?

One question came up early, and it is worth settling before any number goes on a label.

A footprint per litre and a footprint per can are not the same thing, and the gap between them isn't fixed. The same beer in a small can and a large can has the same footprint per litre but a different footprint per can, because a smaller can uses more aluminium for every litre it holds.

So before you publish a figure, decide which one your customers and consumers will actually read. On a can, it is almost always per can, and it should be for the can size the figure is printed on.

## 4. The fastest lever was already known

The good news about a large packaging share is that it is one of the quickest things to change. The footprint of a can depends heavily on how much recycled aluminium goes into it, and can makers increasingly publish that share, because their own customers ask for it.

For Kvarnen, that turned the biggest surprise in the footprint into the easiest next step. The brewery didn't need a new supplier or a new process to understand its options. It needed its can supplier's recycled content, per can format, and a conversation about what the next contract could look like.

## 5. Malt is the slower lever

Malt is harder to move, but it is where the longer term work sits. Its footprint is set in the field and in the maltings: the growing region, the fertiliser, and the heat it takes to dry the malted grain. That last step alone can outweigh the brewhouse.

Kvarnen has bought from the same Swedish maltster for years. Its next step is to replace the database average for malt with the maltster's own figures, starting with kilning energy. That turns a regional average into supplier data, and a number the brewery can stand behind.

## 6. From compliance to advantage

Kvarnen started because a reporting deadline asked it to. What it ended up with was different: the first picture of its products that it trusted, and a short list of conversations with the two suppliers who carry most of the number.

That is what we mean by turning compliance into a competitive advantage. The report still has to be filed. But the same work gives a producer numbers it can put in front of a customer, defend when asked, and use to decide where to act first.

> "We started because we had to report. What we got was the first honest picture of our own beer, and we know which two conversations to have first."
>
> Malte Frisk, Environment Coordinator, Bryggeri Kvarnen

## 7. What other beverage producers can take from it

- **Check your assumptions against a real split.** The part of the process you spend the most energy on is not necessarily the biggest part of your footprint
- **Look at your packaging early.** For canned and bottled drinks, it can be as large as your main ingredient
- **Decide your unit before you publish.** Per litre and per can tell different stories, and the can size matters
- **Ask your container supplier first.** Recycled content is often the fastest lever, and the figure usually exists already
- **Replace averages where the number is biggest.** Supplier data for your main ingredient is slower to get, and worth more

## Key takeaways

- A beverage footprint rarely sits where the producer expects it
- Per litre and per can are different numbers, and a claim should use the one on the label
- The first footprint is a reporting job; what you do with it is the advantage
