## 1. Why this document exists

We have customers now, a year of them, and five people describe Rootline five different ways. Some of those ways are good. This document picks the good ones, writes them down, and gives everyone the same words for the same things. Use it when you write a deck, an email, a blog post or a proposal. If something here is wrong, tell the PMM rather than working around it.

## 2. Positioning statement

**For** food and beverage producers who have to put a carbon number in front of a customer, an auditor or their own board,

**Rootline is** the carbon accounting platform built only for food and beverage,

**that** measures emissions down to the ingredient, by origin and farming practice, and reaches past the first tier of suppliers to the farm.

**Unlike** generalist carbon platforms, which model agriculture as a category average, and spend based tools, which multiply what you paid by an industry factor,

**we** give you a number you can explain line by line, because we know where food emissions actually sit.

## 3. The one sentence version

Rootline is carbon accounting built only for food and beverage, measured at the ingredient, all the way to the farm.

## 4. Where we come from

Rootline started inside a question a Nordic dairy cooperative could not answer: which of our farms, and which of our feed choices, make our milk the number it is. The first version of the model was built to answer that question for that cooperative's own supply chain, before it was ever a product. Everything since has grown out of that foundation. It is why the product goes deep on food and does not pretend to go anywhere else.

Use this story when a buyer asks why we only do food. Do not use it as an opening line.

## 5. Why now (June 2025)

In the order we hear them from buyers:

1. **Customers are asking.** Large brand owners and grocers are sending their suppliers scorecards and templates asking for product level carbon numbers, with a date attached. This is the most common reason an ingredient producer talks to us.
2. **Targets need farm data.** Companies setting a science based target in food find that most of their footprint sits on farms they do not own, and almost nobody has that data.
3. **Deforestation rules put origin on the table.** Soy, palm, cocoa and coffee now come with questions about where exactly they were grown.
4. **Reporting rules are arriving for the larger groups.** Multi brand owners are preparing for their first CSRD reports. This matters, but it is not yet why most of our customers buy.

## 6. Messaging pillars

### 6.1 Built for food

- **Rootline is built only for food and beverage.** Our factors, our data model and our supplier logic all assume agricultural inputs. We do not serve other industries and we say so.
- **In food, one line is the footprint.** For a dairy it is the milk, for a bakery it is the flour, for a brewery it is the barley and the glass. Everything else is a rounding error until that line is right, and most tools spend the first year on everything else.
- **Buyers notice in the first meeting** that we do not need to be taught their industry. Let that happen. Ask about their recipes and their suppliers early.

### 6.2 Measured at the ingredient

- **Ingredient level factors, by origin and farming practice.** The same ingredient from two countries, or grown two ways, is two different numbers. We model the recipe, the origin and the practice, not the spend category.
- **Public data for the baseline, partner data for the detail.** We start from the public life cycle databases everyone uses, and add licensed agricultural data from partners, which is what lets us resolve below the category. Today that is about 2,800 ingredient level factors across the Nordic food base.
- **Land use change, handled properly.** When land was converted to grow something, the carbon released is attributed to what grows there afterwards, spread over twenty years. We model it, flag it on the line it belongs to, and show it when it moves. Most tools either ignore it or hide it in an average.
- **Spend based numbers move with price.** A spend based footprint multiplies what you paid by an industry average. Pay more for a better ingredient and your footprint goes up. That is fine for a first rough picture and useless for a number on a product, a claim or a supplier decision.

### 6.3 All the way to the farm

- **We reach past the first tier.** In food the emissions are not at tier one, they are at the farm, the grower and the vessel. Rootline Supplier goes out to them with a short, structured request, sent in the customer's name rather than ours, chased by the system, and the supplier gets their own number back.
- **Suppliers answer when it is worth their while.** A supplier who gets their own figure back, and can use it with every other buyer who asks, is a supplier who replies.

### 6.4 Numbers that show their quality

- **Every line shows where it came from.** Each line in a footprint is marked as primary data, a regional average or a global average. A report with thirty percent primary data says so, on the face of it.
- **The quality is part of the number.** Say it this way: we do not hide the gaps, we label them.

## 9. The alternatives buyers weigh

| Alternative | What they are good at | What we say |
|---|---|---|
| **Verdanta** (generalist platform) | Every industry, a polished interface, reduction planning | "A good product if food is a third of your footprint. If it is ninety percent, you need someone who does not treat rapeseed oil as rapeseed oil." |
| **Klimatly** (spend based, self serve) | Fast and cheap | "The quickest number available, and the one that moves when your prices move." |
| **Consultancies** (for example Hedberg & Lund) | A finished report, no effort from the customer | "A good deliverable once. We are the answer when you will be asked every year and want to own the model." |
| **Spreadsheets and questionnaires** | Free, and familiar | "Holds until an auditor or a customer asks where a number came from." |

Never run down a competitor by name in writing. Say what they are built for.

## 10. Words

| Say | Avoid | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Carbon accounting for food and beverage | Sustainability platform | Too wide; buyers can't tell what we do |
| Ingredient level | Granular, AI powered | Nobody knows what granular means |
| Product footprint | LCA (unless the buyer says it first) | Most buyers hear "consultant" |
| Primary data, regional average | Estimates | "Estimates" sounds like we made it up |
| Supplier engagement | Supplier surveys | Surveys are what they already fail at |
| Carbon neutral, net zero products | Anything we cannot substantiate | Claims are becoming a legal question |

Buyers rarely say "carbon accounting" on a first call. Expect "Scope 3 tool", "footprint software" or "the LCA thing", and match their words.

## 11. Do not oversell

- **Reduction planning.** We measure well. What to do next is thin today: single product what ifs, nothing more. Do not describe a pathway, a cost curve or a plan.
- **The interface.** It is functional, not beautiful. If a buyer compares screens side by side, we lose on looks. Say so before they do.
- **Anything outside food.** Our factors and supplier logic assume food inputs. If the buyer makes packaging, chemicals or anything else, tell them we are the wrong tool.
