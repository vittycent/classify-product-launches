# GTM Tiering Agent: setup interview

_Draft 0.2, 11 Oct 2026. Rewritten after the first full calibration: eight questions instead of ten, a starting point under every question, and checks that keep the scoring questions from overlapping._

Answer these eight questions about your company before the tiering agent scores anything. Your answers become your launch rubric: the tiers, the scoring questions, what a 0, a 5 and a 10 mean for each one, the weights, the tier lines, and what each tier sets in motion.

**Every question comes with a starting point.** It is a default you can keep, change or replace. Reacting to a default is faster than starting from a blank page, and anything you leave as it is still works. Change whatever doesn't fit your company.

**How to answer.** One question at a time. Talking it through works well, and long answers help. If you dictate, check that the last sentence of each answer is complete before moving on.

**How scoring works once the rubric exists.** For each release, you score it first, before you see the agent's score. The agent scores it from the evidence. You then talk through only the questions where the two scores are 3 or more points apart. Each gap is fixed at its cause, one change per review, so the rubric gets closer to how your business really works.

Under each question, "Why we ask" says what your answer turns into.

---

## 1. Why tier at all?

What goes wrong at your company when every release gets the same launch treatment? Think of one release that got too much launch effort and one that got too little.

- *Starting point:* the most common failure runs one way. A release that took a lot of engineering effort gets a big launch because of that effort, not because customers or the business gain much from it. Effort is not impact.
- *Why we ask:* it states the problem the tiering agent solves for you, and the two releases become your first test cases.

## 2. Your tiers

How many launch tiers do you use, or want to use? Describe each one in a sentence a new product manager would understand, and give one past release as an example of each.

- *Starting point:* four tiers.

| Tier | What it is | Its job |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Gives you an advantage competitors can't easily copy, such as entering a new category, and brings in revenue | Tell the market |
| 2 | Brings in revenue but catches you up with the market: something customers expect | Tell customers and sales |
| 3 | Serves your core customers | Tell the customers it serves |
| 4 | Makes the product better to use: quality, speed, less friction | Log it |

- *Why we ask:* it gives the tier table and the examples the agent compares new releases against.

## 3. The questions you ask, and what counts as proof

When a new release comes up, what do you ask about it to decide how big the launch should be? Work through it in this order:

1. **List your questions.** Short is fine.
2. **Say what each one measures,** in a few words: where the idea came from, how many customers it reaches, how strong the proof is.
3. **Check for overlap.** If two questions measure the same thing, merge them or split the job between them. Overlaps are easy to miss in the wording and easy to see once each question has its 0, 5 and 10.
4. **Check the direction.** A higher score must always mean a bigger launch.
5. **Describe a 0, a 5 and a 10** for each question at your company.
6. **Rank what counts as proof,** strongest first.

- *Starting point, the questions:*

| Question | Measures | 0 | 5 | 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| New category | Whether it opens a new market category | Strengthens our position in our current market | Opens complementary use cases in other markets | Moves us into a new category |
| Market need | Who needs it beyond our current customers | Only the customer who asked | Companies like our customers that we don't serve yet | The market as a whole |
| Validation | What kinds of proof exist; how many asked is a separate question | No proof: a gut feeling; we build it to find out | Two kinds of proof agree, such as requests and a workaround customers pay for | Customers have used it, or several kinds of proof agree |
| Strategic signals | Fit with company goals; how important a release is to the business counts here, through a goal, and nowhere else | No goal names it, directly or through a release it has to ship before | Supports one company goal, directly or by shipping before a release a goal names | Supports several company goals |
| Customer request | Where the idea came from | Our own idea; no customer asked | Our idea, and one or a few customers asked for something like it | Driven by requests from many customers |
| Market expectation | Whether customers expect it in our market | Not expected | Not expected, but customers would be delighted | Expected |
| Customer reach | How many of our current customers can use it | A few customers | More customers, in a few segments | Most of our customers |

- *Starting point, proof, strongest first:* (1) results from customers testing it; (2) deals lost or stalled that named it; (3) workarounds customers pay for, such as a consultancy, extra staff or spreadsheets; (4) requests in calls and emails, counted by customer; (5) signals from inside the company: sales, customer success, support tickets; (6) gut feeling. Compare every release with what customers do without it, not only with competitors.
- *Starting point, how you read them:* if you know you read a question strictly or generously ("I'm bullish on reach, so I score it lower"), say so. The agent's anchors follow your habit, so the two of you disagree about evidence, not about reading.
- *Why we ask:* these become the scoring questions and their anchors. Written anchors are what make two people, or a person and the agent, give the same score. The proof ranking tells the agent what to read and how far to trust it.

## 4. What counts more

Which questions matter most, and why? Is there anything that should move a release up a tier on its own?

- **Give a reason for every weight,** in a sentence you could say to a product manager or a salesperson. The weights are the part of a rubric people ask about first.
- **Before adding a question, ask whether a yes or no gate would do.** Every extra question costs engagement.
- *Starting point, weights* (they add up to 10, so the total is out of 100):

| Question | Weight | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| New category | 2 | A Tier 1 launch rests on an advantage competitors can't easily copy, and a new category is the clearest sign of one |
| Market need | 2 | A big launch needs a market beyond the customers you already have |
| Validation | 1.5 | The stronger the proof, the more you can say in public without overpromising |
| Strategic signals | 1.5 | A release that moves a company goal deserves more |
| Customer request | 1 | Requests matter, but one loud request alone shouldn't make a big launch |
| Market expectation | 1 | Catching up still needs telling, but it is worth half of breaking new ground |
| Customer reach | 1 | How many current customers can use it |

- *Starting point, gates:* **development maturity** (an idea, ready to test with customers, ready for everyone): below "ready to test", the tier is provisional and the release is scored again later. Maturity says when to tier, not how big. **Revenue:** a release can only be Tier 1 or 2 if it opens a new revenue stream or helps win or expand deals; otherwise Tier 3 at most.
- *Starting point, overrides:* a big deal that depends on the release, or a date in the market customers must meet, moves it up one tier, never more and never down. Whether a deal depends on the release is judged by the newest thing anyone has said about the deal, in the CRM, an email or a call: deals change in conversations before the CRM catches up. A competitor launching the same thing changes the message (catching up, not first), not the tier. The score itself stays as it is; you approve each override, and it is logged with its reason.
- *Why we ask:* it sets the weights, the gates and the overrides.

## 5. Where the lines fall

If the total is out of 100, where do the lines between tiers fall, and what happens to a release that lands right on a line?

- *Starting point:* Tier 1 at 70 or more, Tier 2 at 50 to 69, Tier 3 at 30 to 49, Tier 4 under 30. Within 3 points of a line, the agent flags it and you decide; the release stays in the lower tier unless the reason to move up is clear and written down. Every tiering system leans towards Tier 1, and Tier 1 and 2 launches cost a lot of effort, so watch how many releases land there.
- *Why we ask:* it sets the tier lines and the rule for borderline releases.

## 6. Where the answers live

Where does the information for these questions live at your company, what is each source trusted for, and which one wins when two disagree? Include where the business signals come from: company goals, big deals, dates in the market, competitor launches. Name every place evidence lives, not only the main tools: call recordings, email, internal meetings, team chat, the support desk, the feedback portal. The agent reads only what you name here.

- *Starting point, one job per source:* the roadmap tool for what is planned; project updates and the product team's chat for what is really happening; call transcripts and emails for customer evidence; a pipeline export for deals; shared files for company goals (three to five for the year, each with a measure and an owner, read by the goal setter too) and for dates in the market; the roadmap watch agent or competitor notes for competitor launches.
- *Starting point, when sources disagree:* (1) the newest dated statement from the person who owns the item wins; (2) a newer conversation beats the roadmap tool, because what is really happening lives in conversation; (3) an automatic summary never beats its source; (4) if the disagreement would change the tier, the agent says so and asks, instead of picking quietly.
- *Why we ask:* it tells the agent what to read, what to trust it for, and what to do when sources conflict.

## 7. What a tier sets in motion

Once a release has a tier, what happens next: how often, how much lead time, who leads, who hears about it, and which assets?

- *Starting point,* drawn from what product marketing leaders publish about their own launches:

| Tier | How often | Lead time | Who leads | Who hears about it | Assets |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | About once a quarter | 8 to 10 weeks | Product marketing, with the product manager; leadership signs off | New buyers, press, analysts and every customer | Positioning and messaging with the sales deck updated; a dedicated landing page; press; analyst briefings where analysts matter; sales training; a campaign (email, social from the company and its leaders, paid); a launch moment; a demo video; customer proof; in app education; a customer success briefing; 30 days of follow up promotion |
| 2 | One or two a month, combined into the quarter's story where possible | About 4 weeks | Product marketing, with the product manager | Existing customers and current pipeline | Blog post; email to the customers it serves; sales one pager and a short briefing; social; in app announcement; changelog; an update to an existing web page |
| 3 | As needed | 2 weeks | Product manager; product marketing approves | The core customers it serves | Release note; email to those customers; customer success briefing; in app note |
| 4 | As needed | Ship day | Product manager | Customers who use the feature | Changelog line; a mention in the monthly product update |

- *Why we ask:* it gives the trigger table, which is the handoff to the brief and the launch plan. In this kit the agents run Tier 3 and 4 launches and prepare Tier 1 and 2, so product marketing has time for the story.

## 8. Who decides, and when to look again

Who makes the final call on a tier, and in what order do people weigh in? What should make you score a release again? When your score and the agent's differ, how do you settle it?

- *Starting point, who decides:* product marketing scores first, then gets the product manager's input, then decides. Tier 1 also needs leadership sign off.
- *Starting point, when to score again:* scope is added or cut; the date moves into another quarter; results come in from customers testing it; it becomes ready to test with customers; a big deal or a market date appears or falls away; a competitor launches the same thing. Each score is dated and kept, never overwritten.
- *Starting point, after a release ships:* at every quarterly calibration, score again every release from the last two quarters with its results; one that moves up a tier gets the launch it missed.
- *Starting point, when scores differ:* only gaps of 3 or more points on a question are discussed, about 10 per review. Each is fixed at its cause: the agent missed a source (add the source), an anchor was read two ways (rewrite the anchor), or a weight feels wrong (change the weight). One change per review, a version number on the rubric, and a log of every change. Once a quarter, compare tiers with how the launches actually did, so the rubric learns from results and not only from the person scoring.
- *Why we ask:* it sets the review step, what triggers a new score, and how calibration works.
